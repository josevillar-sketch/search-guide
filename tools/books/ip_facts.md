## C. Time for the task

1. At least 30 school days. Testing days, breaks and holidays do not count. Talks happen after the 30 days. Source: CED p. 46, https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap-seminar-course-and-exam-description.pdf . Words: "Students must be given at least 30 school days (state testing, spring breaks, holidays, etc. would not count toward those days) to complete their research, compose their essays, and develop their presentations. The actual presentations must take place outside the 30-day window."
2. More time is not better. **[A]** Source: CED p. 46. Words: "Teachers would disadvantage students by giving them less time. Giving more time could also disadvantage students by reducing the time available for completing the Team Project and Presentation."
3. Room 63 arithmetic, not a College Board statement: Seminar meets every other school day, so 30 school days is 15 class meetings for each period.

## D. What PT2 is, its parts and weights

1. PT2 is 35 percent of the AP Seminar score. Source: EXAM, https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/courses/ap-seminar/exam . Words: "Performance Task 2: Individual Research-Based Essay and Presentation—35% of AP Seminar Score".
2. The three parts. Source: EXAM. Words: "Individual written argument (2,000 words) / College Board scored / 70% of 35%", "Individual multimedia presentation (6–8 minutes) / Teacher scored / 20% of 35%", "Oral defense (2 questions from the teacher) / Teacher scored / 10% of 35%".
3. Arithmetic on those rows: IWA 24.5 percent of the AP score, IMP 7 percent, OD 3.5 percent, IMP and OD together 10.5 percent (Presenting and Defending already says 10.5).
4. The rest of the AP score, for context. **[A]** PT1 is 20 percent. The End-of-Course Exam is 2 hours and 45 percent: Part A, 3 short-answer questions (suggested 30 minutes, 30% of 45%), Part B, 1 evidence-based argument essay (suggested 90 minutes, 70% of 45%). Source: EXAM. Words: "End-of-Course Exam (2 Hours)—45% of AP Seminar Score", "Understanding and analyzing an argument (3 short-answer questions); suggested time: 30 minutes / College Board scored / 30% of 45%", "Evidence-Based argument essay (1 long essay); suggested time: 90 minutes / College Board scored / 70% of 45%".
5. The task in one sentence. Source: CED p. 46. Words: "Students then compose a research question of their own; conduct research; analyze, evaluate, and select evidence to develop an argument; and present and defend their conclusions."
6. The handout's table. Source: PKT26 p. 1, https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap26-seminar-task-2-stimulus-materials.pdf . Words: "Individual Written Argument (IWA) 2,000 words / Individual Multimedia Presentation (IMP) 6–8 minutes / Oral Defense (OD) Respond to 2 questions".
7. The College Board scores the IWA. The teacher scores the IMP and OD after required training. Source: STU, https://apstudents.collegeboard.org/courses/ap-seminar/assessment . Words: "AP Seminar teachers also take part in mandatory training from the AP Program in how to score these components." **[A]** POL: "all AP Seminar and AP Research teachers need to complete and pass online scoring training annually to obtain access to score their students' work."
8. Where each part lives. The IWA is uploaded to the AP Digital Portfolio. The IMP and OD are live and have no upload. The exam is in Bluebook. Source: EXAM. Words: "two performance tasks submitted through the AP Digital Portfolio and the end-of-course AP Exam."

## G. Finding a theme and writing your own question

1. Read for connections. Source: PKT26 p. 2. Words: "Read and analyze the provided stimulus materials to identify thematic connections among the sources and possible areas for inquiry."
2. The theme must link at least two texts. Source: PKT26 p. 2. Words: "Your question must relate to a theme that connects at least two of the stimulus materials."
3. The question should suit your interest and be rich enough. Source: PKT26 p. iv. Words: "develop a research question that suits your individual interest based on a thematic connection between at least two of the stimulus materials. Your research question must be rich enough to allow you to engage in meaningful exploration and to write and present a substantive, defensible argument."
4. Strong papers found themes rooted in two or more texts, even outside the named theme. Weak ones took a topic from one text only, or a topic a text already argues. Source: CRR25 p. 6. Words: "Discovered themes that were clearly rooted in at least two texts in the stimulus packet, including topics outside of the overarching theme of 'memory/nostalgia.'" / "Adopted a topic already presented in one of the stimulus sources that effectively turned the response into a summary/report instead of an argument." / "Selected a topic from one stimulus source that is not covered or discussed in another source, which failed to highlight a theme that connects at least two sources."
5. Start with the obvious, then look deeper. Source: CRR25 p. 10. Words: "Begin with acknowledging the obvious, low-hanging fruit and then push students to seek less-obvious connections."
6. The question is your own. The teacher may not give one. Source: PKT26 p. 2 and CED p. 49. Words: "Compose a research question of your own prompted by analysis of the stimulus materials." DO NOT: "Assign, provide, distribute, or generate research questions for students."
7. **[C]** A tool may not hand you your question uncritically. The phase table's not-acceptable use keeps a qualifier: it bans taking a tool's question or thesis without doing the research, not every mention of a question. Source: CED p. 37 and PKT26 p. 5. Words: "Taking the output of generative AI tools uncritically, such as using AI to generate a research question or thesis, without engaging with the actual research or relying solely on generative AI as a source of information about a topic". Quote it with "uncritically" and "without engaging with the actual research" kept in.
8. Keep refining the question. Source: PKT26 p. 2. Words: "Throughout your research, continually revisit and refine your original research question to ensure that the evidence you gather addresses your purpose and focus."
9. Weak questions, from the Chief Reader. Source: CRR25 pp. 7 to 8. Words: "Asked more than one question or broached multiple topics." / "Failed to situate the topic, problem, issue, or concern in a particular time or place. For example, 'Why are memories important?' as opposed to 'How is nostalgia leveraged in the US to influence consumer behavior?'" / "Asked a question that is closed, irrelevant, or superfluous and does not invite debate". **[A]** Also: "Provided a broad research question or thesis that oversimplified perspectives, claims, or conclusions." / "Failed to convey the urgency or impact of the question in relation to why the topic matters or who will be affected".
10. A good question. Source: CRR25 p. 11. Words: "Demonstrate the importance of a good research question (open, relevant, debatable, researchable, etc.)".
11. The IWA argues. The IRR reported. Source: CRR25 p. 11. Words: "the IRR uses research to report on, explain, and offer information about a topic; the IWA uses research to contextualize a topic, defend a position, support an argument, illuminate a perspective, etc."

## H. Research and Checkpoint 1 (Process and Sources)

1. Research beyond the packet must include several perspectives and scholarly work. Source: PKT26 p. 2. Words: "Gather information from a range of additional sources representing a variety of perspectives, including scholarly work." and "You should include evidence from scholarly work."
2. Checkpoint 1 is required for the IWA and happens during research. Source: CED p. 51, https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap-seminar-course-and-exam-description.pdf . Words: "Required for IRR and IWA" / "Students submit to the teacher a log of the sources they have found and read, together with their notes/annotations/comments on them. Teachers can determine the precise format of this log, for example, an annotated bibliography, a source chart, or digital annotations on sources. The log should include links to the original sources where possible." / "Occurs during the research phase of the task".
3. The talk. Source: CED p. 51. Words: "Teachers hold a short conversation with each student about their research process, the evolution of their research question, the usefulness of the sources they have found (as documented in their source log) or the perspectives they represent."
4. The student's version. Source: PKT26 p. 2. Words: "you will be required to submit evidence of the original sources that you have found and read to your teacher." / "your teacher will arrange a time for you to discuss your research and sources with them. For that discussion you should be prepared to talk about your sources, and the perspectives and ideas you have found in your research."
5. Passing means saying more than the log. Not passing means a redo. A suspicion of AI use is talked through first. Source: CED p. 51. Words: "Successful completion of this checkpoint means a student is able to discuss this phase of research in a manner that extends beyond mere repetition of the information in their source log." / "should be given the opportunity to repeat the process (with new source log and conversation)." / "If there is suspicion that the student has relied on generative AI without reading or understanding the original sources, that should be discussed with the student before they re-do the process."
6. **[A]** The teacher sets the checkpoint dates. Affirmations are required for a score. Source: CED p. 51. Words: "Teachers can schedule the checkpoints as they deem appropriate, but where possible they should occur during the relevant phase of the task." and "Teacher affirmations are required for a student to receive a score for that task."
7. Why checkpoints exist. Source: CED p. 51. Words: "To ensure students aren't using generative AI to bypass work, students must complete interim 'checkpoints' with their teacher to demonstrate genuine engagement with the performance tasks." POL calls them "short conversations with students during which students make their thinking and decision-making visible (similar to an oral defense)."
8. EBSCO and Turnitin are free through the AP Digital Portfolio. Source: IG p. 7. Words: "all AP Seminar and AP Research students will receive digital access to EBSCO® Academic Search Ultimate and Turnitin via the AP Digital Portfolio at no additional cost." SUG p. 17: "EBSCO's research databases can be accessed by clicking on the Overview menu item under the Class Summary."
9. Source mistakes the Chief Reader named. Source: CRR25 p. 9. Words: "Selected evidence primarily from news organizations, popular magazines, blogs, online encyclopedic sources, or social media sites." / "Relied on a limited number of sources (often featuring one or two throughout the entirety of the paper)" / "Introduced ideas or concepts from sources that are too shallow to be effective, suggesting the writer read little more than the abstract or the first paragraph."

## K. IWA rules

1. 2,000 words. What counts. Source: PKT26 p. 2. Words: "Abide by the 2,000-word limit (excluding footnoted citations, bibliography, and text in figures or tables). Word count does include titles, sub-headings, and in-text citations."
2. What the argument must do. Source: PKT26 p. 2. 6. Ways to cite. Source: PKT26 p. 1. Words: "Acknowledge, attribute, and/or cite sources using in-text citations, endnotes or footnotes, and/or through bibliographic entry."
7. Remove your name, school and teacher. The final file is a PDF. Source: PKT26 p. 2 and SUG p. 6. Words: "Remove references to your name, school and teacher." / "Remove your name from written work prior to submitting it as final." / "Written work being submitted as final must be in PDF format."
8. Off-topic and zero rules. Source: SG26 p. 2. Words: "if the response is not in any way related to a theme connecting at least two of the stimulus materials, it is off-topic and a score of OT is assigned. Zero points are given to off-topic responses." / "Scores of 0 are assigned to all rows of the rubric when the response is a repetition of a prompt; entirely crossed-out; a drawing or other markings; a presentation (or other off-task format); or a response in a language other than English."
9. **[A]** Readers lean toward scoring. Source: SG26 p. 2. Words: "readers should reward the student who derives their ideas from at least two of the stimulus materials, even if they wandered away from them as they pursued their topic." / "If you can discern any possible connection to a theme derived from two or more stimulus materials, the response should be scored." / "A READER SHOULD NEVER SCORE A PAPER AS OFF-TOPIC. INSTEAD, PLACE THE RESPONSE IN TEMP HOLD AND DISCUSS WITH YOUR TABLE LEADER."
10. Each row is scored on its own. Source: SG26 p. 2. Words: "award the score for that row based solely upon the criteria indicated for that row, according to the preponderance of evidence."

## Y. Do not state as fact (and what to say instead)

1. **A day in January for the packet.** No day is published. Never write "January 31" (that is the Course Audit deadline, and a web AI summary repeated this error on September 30, 2026). Say "January 2027" or "early January", and put the Room 63 release day in a WAITING card.
2. **The 2027 packet: theme, texts, how many.** Unknown until January. Say "the last three packets each had 7 texts, but no rule fixes the number". Do not predict a theme.
3. **That the 2027 rubric will match 2026.** Likely (same shape three years, CED unchanged), but 2027 guidelines are published only after the exam. Say "the 2026 scoring guidelines, the newest posted", and "Check the College Board page" in a note.
4. **That 2027 readers will score an old-packet paper off-topic.** Say: the CED requires this year's packet, and the 2025 guidelines told readers to treat an old-packet-only paper as off-topic.
5. **A penalty for going over 2,000 words.** None is stated. Two 48-point released samples list student-typed counts of 2,198 and 2,232. Say only "stay within 2,000 words" and what counts. Do not mention the samples as permission.
6. **A penalty for a talk under 6 minutes.** None is stated. Say "6 to 8 minutes; only the first 8 count".
7. **Whether a missed checkpoint zeroes all of PT2.** The CED ties the checkpoints to the IWA score, and no IWA means no IMP or OD score. But the College Board never says outright that an IWA zeroed for a missed checkpoint also removes the IMP and OD. Say "a missed checkpoint scores 0 on the task, and you cannot get an IWA score without both checkpoints". Stop there.
8. **What follows a "No" or "Questionable" authenticity answer** (a 0, an investigation, an appeal). Not spelled out for students. Say "I file a report, and the College Board may ask for your interim work".
9. **How raw points turn into the weights** (IMP 36 points = 20 percent, OD 12 points = 10 percent). Not published. Give the weights and the points separately. Do not show a conversion.
10. **Reusing your PT1 issue, sources or lens in PT2.** No rule speaks to it. The rules require a theme from two of this year's texts and ban repurposing papers, even your own. Put the Room 63 rule in a WAITING card.
11. **Help allowed during a practice PT2.** A Room 63 decision (WAITING card).
12. **"AI is allowed for four things only."** Wrong. See Q2 and Q5.
13. **"AI may not help with your question at all."** Too strong. The ban is on taking a tool's question or thesis uncritically, without the research (G7).
14. **"You will see your AI score in Turnitin."** Wrong. Students see only the originality report (P6).
15. **"No one takes the exam on paper."** Approved accommodations can mean a paper exam. Say "fully digital in Bluebook".
16. **Button names on a Room 63 iPad.** From the Summer 2026 student guide, not checked on a Room 63 iPad. Keep The Team Project's caveat.
